Pulmonary Segmentation
Shanghai Jiao Tong University · Research · Jan 2023 – May 2024
A 3D U-Net pipeline for pulmonary vessel and airway segmentation from thoracic CT, with topology-aware losses to keep thin structures connected.
The problem
Pulmonary vessels and airways in thoracic CT are thin, branching trees. A segmentation that looks fine in voxel overlap can still be wrong in the way that matters clinically: a broken centerline, a leak into nearby tissue, or a missing distal branch. Standard 3D models trained only with region overlap will take those shortcuts. Filling a gap with a blob, or cutting a vessel where contrast is weak, barely moves Dice and completely changes the topology.
Full-resolution 3D volumes are also awkward to train on. GPU memory pushes you toward patches, while the structure you care about spans many slices. This project combined 2D preprocessing, patch-based 3D training, volumetric reconstruction, and topology-aware supervision so the reconstructed trees stayed connected instead of only looking filled-in.
2D–3D pipeline
I built a 3D U-Net pipeline that does not throw the volume at the network raw. 2D slice-level preprocessing handles per-slice normalization and cleanup before 3D patch-based training sees the data. Patches make training feasible; volumetric reconstruction stitches predictions back into a full scan. Data augmentation had to respect anatomy — intensity and geometric variation that still looks like CT — rather than generic image flips that break left/right lung structure.
I also used 2D–3D projection in two directions. Qualitatively, projecting 3D predictions down to 2D made it much easier to see leakage and broken branches than scrolling a voxel cube. Practically, the same projection helped reconstruct volumes from 2D segmentation outputs, so 2D signals could still inform the 3D result instead of living in a separate demo.
Topology-aware training
Baseline predictions failed in two characteristic ways: leakage, where the mask bled off the vessel, and discontinuity, where a thin branch disappeared for a few slices and the tree split. A connectivity-preserving loss penalizes those breaks directly, instead of waiting for voxel Dice to notice. If the centerline is gone, the loss should move, even when most voxels in a thick trunk are still correct.
I evaluated with clDice (centerline Dice) in addition to region overlap. clDice compares skeletons, so a fat, leaky mask that covers the vessel but also the surroundings scores worse than a thinner mask that follows the true path. That metric is what made topology a first-class objective rather than something we only inspected in screenshots.
Results & stack
The pipeline produced vessel and airway segmentations from thoracic CT by combining 2D preprocessing, 3D U-Net patches, volumetric reconstruction, and topology-aware supervision. Connectivity-preserving loss and clDice were aimed at the failure modes voxel Dice hides: leakage and broken centerlines.
